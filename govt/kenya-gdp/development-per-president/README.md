# Kenya development spending under Kibaki, Kenyatta and Ruto, adjusted for debt

## 1. Why this exists

A Stats Kenya chart ("Development spending has halved as a share of GDP")
shows national government **development spending as a share of GDP**,
FY2003/04 to FY2025/26: Kibaki 5.0%, Kenyatta 5.9%, Ruto 3.6% (averages of
yearly values). That image belongs to Stats Kenya and is not included here.
`kenya-devt-spending-pct-gdp.png` is an independent reproduction of it from
primary data.

This is an independent analysis. It is not affiliated with or endorsed by Stats
Kenya, the Central Bank of Kenya, the IMF or the World Bank.

**The criticism:** the chart ignores debt. A president can borrow heavily to
fund development, and the next one inherits the interest and repayments, which
leaves less money for new projects.

This repo:

1. **Reproduces the % of GDP chart** from primary data (CBK + IMF GDP).
2. Measures development spending as a **share of revenue left after interest**:
   first after all interest, then (the fairer version) after only the interest
   on debt each president **inherited**.
3. Measures whether borrowing paid for **day-to-day (recurrent) spending**, which
   leaves debt for the next president with no asset behind it.
4. Measures **borrowing per term**, both from the fiscal deficit and from the
   change in the debt stock.

**How to read "Kibaki", "Kenyatta", "Ruto".** These are shorthand for the
national government's fiscal outcomes in the financial years assigned to each
president (section 4). Budgets are passed by Parliament, and outcomes also
reflect economic conditions such as the 2008–09 global financial crisis and
COVID-19 in 2020/21, not only the choices of the government of the day.

## 2. Findings

### Which measure is fairest?

No single chart is fair on its own. Read these two together:

| | Kibaki | Kenyatta | Ruto |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Development effort:** dev ÷ (revenue − inherited interest), avg of years | 33.5 | 39.3 | 28.5 |
| same, pooled (term total ÷ term total) | 38.2 | 37.9 | 28.5 |
| **Burden passed on:** recurrent balance, % of revenue − interest | +13.8 | −10.7 | −21.3 |

- **Ruto years:** lowest on development effort, even after allowing for
  inherited interest. Also the largest share of borrowing going to recurrent
  spending.
- **Kenyatta years:** the lead over the Kibaki years on development mostly
  disappears once interest on debt taken on during the term isn't credited back.
  Recurrent balance negative from 15/16 onward.
- **Kibaki years:** revenue covered recurrent spending every year, and borrowing
  was less than development spending.

Why not the others:

- **% of GDP** ignores debt.
- **After all interest** favours whoever borrows. A president's interest on his
  own new borrowing is deducted as if he inherited it.
- **After interest + principal** double-counts, because most principal is
  refinanced with new loans.

Two caveats apply to everything: county development spending since 2013 is not
counted (section 2.5c), and the inherited-interest split is approximate
(section 5).

All per-president figures are in `kenya_by_president.csv`, as both the
**average of yearly values** (the chart's method, used in the tables below) and
the **pooled** value (term total ÷ term total). Yearly data:
`kenya_dev_vs_net_revenue.csv`.

### 2.2 Development ÷ (revenue − all interest)

Formula: `development expenditure / (tax revenue + non-tax revenue − domestic interest − foreign interest)`

| President | Interest as % of revenue | Dev ÷ (rev − interest) |
|---|---|---|
| Kibaki | 12% | **34.9%** |
| Kenyatta | 21% | **44.8%** |
| Ruto | 32% | **30.7%** |

Chart: `kenya-devt-spending-after-interest.png`.

- **Interest explains part of the gap.** Interest took about 11–13% of revenue under
  Kibaki, about 26–28% at the end of the Kenyatta years, and about 34% in
  2024/25–2025/26.
- **The ranking matches the GDP chart.** The Ruto years have the smallest
  share going to development (29–35% a year).
- **The loophole.** All interest is deducted, including interest on the
  president's own new borrowing. Kenyatta inherited 1.85tn of debt and left
  8.58tn, yet every shilling of interest in 21/22 is treated as a burden he
  inherited. Section 2.3 fixes this.

### 2.3 Development ÷ (revenue − inherited interest) — fairest development comparison

Formula: `development / (revenue − interest × debt at start of term ÷ debt at start of FY)`

| President | All interest, % of revenue | **Inherited** interest, % of revenue | Dev ÷ (rev − inherited int), avg of years | Pooled |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kibaki | 11.7 | 8.8 | **33.5** | 38.2 |
| Kenyatta | 21.0 | 9.5 | **39.3** | 37.9 |
| Ruto | 31.9 | 26.7 | **28.5** | 28.5 |

Chart: `kenya-devt-spending-after-inherited-interest.png`.

- **In the Kenyatta years, most interest was on debt taken on during the
  term.** Inherited interest averaged 9.5% of revenue, against 21.0% for all
  interest. In the Ruto years, most was on inherited debt: 26.7% against 31.9%.
- Kenyatta's lead over Kibaki shrinks from 10 points to 6. On the pooled basis
  it disappears (38.2 vs 37.9). Ruto stays lowest.
- The inherited-interest line generally falls through each term, because the inherited
  stock becomes a smaller share of the growing debt. It jumps at each handover,
  when the successor inherits everything.
- **Approximation.** This assumes old and new debt cost the same. Interest
  costs also depend on interest rates at the time, which this split does not
  separate out.

### 2.4 Recurrent balance: did borrowing pay for day-to-day spending?

Formula: `(revenue + grants − recurrent spending − county transfers) / (revenue − interest)`.
Recurrent spending includes interest.

This is algebraically the same as `(development − deficit) / (revenue − interest)`,
because total spending = recurrent + county transfers + development. It can look
like "development paid for from own money", but **development spending cancels
out**, so this measures fiscal discipline, not development effort. A president who spent nothing on development would score the
same as one who spent heavily, if their recurrent spending was the same.

| President | Recurrent balance, avg of years | Pooled |
|---|---|---|
| Kibaki | **+13.8** | +13.6 |
| Kenyatta | **−10.7** | −11.9 |
| Ruto | **−21.3** | −21.5 |

Chart: `kenya-recurrent-balance.png`.

- **Below zero** means borrowing paid for part of recurrent spending. That leaves
  debt for the next president with no asset behind it, which is the core of the
  fairness complaint.
- Positive in every Kibaki year. In the Kenyatta years, positive only in 13/14
  and 14/15 and negative from 15/16 (lowest: −31 in 20/21, the COVID-19 year).
  Negative in every Ruto year so far (−18 to −27).
- Borrowing for recurrent spending in a downturn (such as 2020/21) can be a
  deliberate choice to support the economy. This measure shows that it happened,
  not whether it was justified.

### 2.5 Other variants

| Measure | Kibaki | Kenyatta | Ruto |
|---|---|---|---|
| c. dev ÷ (rev − interest − county transfers) | 34.9 | 60.5 | **39.6** |
| d. dev ÷ (rev + grants − interest) | 33.1 | 43.7 | 30.3 |

- **(c) County transfers.** Since 2013 a share of revenue goes to counties by
  law. Taking it out of the denominator **moves Ruto above Kibaki**. This is not
  like-for-like either: before 2013 the national government paid for the work
  counties do now, and county development spending is not in the numerator (see
  section 6).
- **(d) Grants.** The numerator includes grant-funded projects, so adding grants
  to the denominator is the consistent version. It changes little.

### 2.6 Borrowing per term

| President | Debt, start → end | Debt added | Deficits (sum) | Development | Debt added ÷ dev | **Deficit ÷ dev** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kibaki | 0.70tn → 1.85tn | 1.15tn | 1.14tn | 1.73tn | 0.67 | **0.66** |
| Kenyatta | 1.85tn → 8.58tn | 6.73tn | 5.89tn | 4.64tn | 1.45 | **1.27** |
| Ruto | 8.58tn → 13.10tn | 4.52tn | 3.98tn | 2.34tn | 1.93 | **1.70** |

Yearly data: `kenya_net_borrowing.csv`.

- **Deficit = total expenditure − revenue − grants** (CBK, commitment basis). It
  is the preferred measure of borrowing because it has no exchange-rate effect.
  The change in the debt stock also includes revaluation of foreign debt. For
  example, in 22/23 the debt rose by 1.70tn against a deficit of 0.84tn because
  the shilling weakened, and in 23/24 it rose by only 0.28tn against a deficit of
  0.88tn because the shilling strengthened.
- On either measure, borrowing was less than development spending in the
  Kibaki years and more in the Kenyatta and Ruto years. Borrowing per shilling
  of development is highest in the Ruto years.

### 2.7 Sensitivity checks (`kenya_sensitivities.csv`)

Values shown: dev % GDP / dev ÷ (rev − all interest) / dev ÷ (rev − inherited interest) / recurrent balance.

| Case | Kibaki | Kenyatta | Ruto |
|---|---|---|---|
| Baseline | 5.0 / 34.9 / 33.5 / 13.8 | 5.9 / 44.8 / 39.3 / −10.7 | 3.6 / 30.7 / 28.5 / −21.3 |
| KSh160bn moved from 13/14 back to 12/13 | 4.7 / 32.7 / 31.4 / 13.8 | 6.3 / 46.9 / 41.4 / −10.7 | unchanged |
| FY given to the president who passed its budget (13/14 → Kibaki, 22/23 → Kenyatta) | 5.0 / 34.9 / 33.3 / 12.6 | 5.8 / 44.2 / 39.1 / −13.0 | 3.6 / 31.1 / 30.3 / −21.7 |

In every case the order is Kenyatta > Kibaki > Ruto on the development ratios
and Kibaki > Kenyatta > Ruto on recurrent balance. Moving the 160bn leaves the
recurrent balance unchanged, as expected: it shifts development spending and the
deficit by the same amount. The exceptions to the ranking are the pooled
inherited-interest figure (2.3) and the county-adjusted measure (2.5c). The
KSh160bn figure is taken from the footnote of the original Stats Kenya chart
("Audited accounts record about KSh 160bn of FY 2012/13 spending in FY 2013/14;
totals over the two years agree").

### 2.8 Principal repayments (illustration only)

We **do not** subtract principal in the main measures. Most maturing debt is
rolled over, and net borrowing is already captured by the deficit (2.6) and the
recurrent balance (2.4). As an illustration, `principal.py` also subtracts
**external** principal (World Bank IDS `DT.AMT.DEPS.CD`, converted at the World
Bank average KSh/US$, FY = mean of two calendar years, FY25/26 uses 2025 only):

| President | Interest + ext. principal, % of revenue | Dev ÷ (rev − int − ext. principal) |
|---|---|---|
| Kibaki | 17% | 37.0% |
| Kenyatta | 28% | 49.3% |
| Ruto | 45% | 38.4% |

Chart: `kenya-devt-spending-after-interest-and-principal.png`. Much external
principal is refinanced with new borrowing, so this double-counts. Domestic principal is left out
because it is mostly rolled-over T-bills and bonds.

## 4. How the numbers were calculated

- **Financial year.** July–June. CBK rows are running totals within the year,
  so we use the **June** row. The year column is the calendar year, so `2026,6` =
  FY2025/26.
- **Revenue** = "TOTAL REVENUE" (tax + non-tax, no grants). **Interest** =
  domestic + foreign interest. **Development** = "DEVELOPMENT EXPENDITURE"
  (county transfers excluded, as in the original chart). **Deficit** = TOTAL
  EXPENDITURE − TOTAL REVENUE − total grants.
- **GDP for an FY** = mean of the two calendar years it spans.
- **Which president.** The president in office for most of the FY. This is
  defined once, in `TERMS` in `common.py`: Kibaki 03/04–12/13, Kenyatta
  13/14–21/22, Ruto 22/23–26/27. New years are assigned automatically. Any FY
  after 26/27 raises an error until the 2027 election result is added.
- **Averages.** The "average of yearly values" matches the chart. The pooled
  value is also in `kenya_by_president.csv`.

## 5. Caveats and limitations

1. **12/13 and 13/14 timing.** About KSh160bn of 12/13 spending was booked in
   13/14 (Stats Kenya chart footnote). The main figures are unadjusted. See 2.7.
2. **County development is missing.** Since 2013 counties must spend at least
   30% of their budgets on development, and none of it is counted here. Results
   that compare across 2013 are not like-for-like. See 2.5c.
3. **Inherited interest is approximate.** It assumes new debt costs the same as
   old debt. Interest costs also depend on interest rates at the time, not
   only the size of the debt. See 2.3.
4. **Commitment basis.** CBK expenditure is on a commitment basis, so deficits
   can differ from Treasury cash-basis figures.
5. **Data revisions.** FY25/26 is CBK's latest data, and 2026 GDP is an IMF
   projection. Both may change.
6. **Spending, not results.** None of these measures say whether the spending
   was good value. A borrowed shilling that built a useful road also leaves an
   asset.

  (*Budget Review and Outlook Paper*, *Quarterly Economic and Budgetary Review*).
